The Brief

Paid traffic was arriving. It just wasn't converting.

NBI was running paid search across more than a dozen state CLE campaigns. Clicks were healthy and cost per click was reasonable — but the pages those clicks landed on weren't turning visitors into registrations.

Rather than guess at a fix, I rebuilt the page around one clear hypothesis and put it head-to-head against the original in a live split test.

What Wasn't Working

Four things standing between a click and a registration.

I audited the existing template against the one thing a paid visitor needs: a fast, obvious path from the ad they clicked to the thing they wanted.

01
The ad promise didn't match the page
Ads referenced a specific state's CLE deadline, but the landing page opened with generic national copy. That mismatch costs trust in the first two seconds and drives bounces on paid traffic you already paid for.
02
Deadline buried below the fold
The compliance deadline is the single strongest motivator for this audience — and it appeared halfway down the page. The most persuasive fact on the page was the one visitors were least likely to see.
03
Too many fields in the form
The registration form asked for information that wasn't needed to start. Every additional field is another reason to abandon, especially on mobile where a majority of this traffic landed.
04
Competing calls to action
Browse the catalog, download a guide, register now — all presented with equal weight. When everything is a CTA, nothing is. Paid landing pages need one job.
The Test

One hypothesis, tested honestly.

Hypothesis: if the page matches the ad's state and deadline, surfaces urgency above the fold, and reduces the form to essentials only, more paid visitors will complete a registration.

Strategy

Match the ad. Remove the friction. Ask once.

01Match message to ad
02Deadline above the fold
03Cut the form down
04One CTA, repeated
05Test, don't assume
01 — Message Match
The page finishes the sentence the ad started
Each state campaign got a page whose headline named that state and its actual deadline. When the page confirms what the ad promised, the visitor stops evaluating whether they're in the right place and starts evaluating the offer.
02 — Urgency Placement
Lead with the deadline, because it's the real motivator
Attorneys don't buy CLE because they want to. They buy because a compliance date is approaching. Moving that date into the headline puts the strongest reason to act in the first thing anyone reads.
03 — Form Friction
Ask for two fields, not five
Everything not required to start was cut or deferred to a later step. On mobile especially, each removed field is a removed reason to abandon. The form also moved above the fold so it's visible without scrolling.
04 — Single Action
One CTA, repeated, with no competition
Browse and download were removed entirely. A paid landing page has one job, and every additional option splits attention and dilutes the result you're paying for.
05 — Validation
Run it live rather than argue about it
The redesign ran against the original at an even traffic split for six weeks. A real test on real paid traffic settles the question in a way that internal opinion never does — and gives a repeatable template for the remaining campaigns.

Design Decisions

Every change had a conversion reason behind it.

Nothing was changed for aesthetics alone — each decision was tied to a specific drop-off point.

Headline
State and date, written out
Naming the state and the actual deadline turns a generic page into one that feels built for that visitor — which is exactly what paid traffic should feel like.
Form Position
Above the fold, not below the pitch
Visitors arriving from a deadline-driven ad are often ready immediately. Making them scroll to act adds friction for the highest-intent segment.
Trust Signals
Approval and volume, stated plainly
State approval is the first objection for CLE buyers. Answering it inline — alongside years in business and review volume — removes the doubt without a separate section.
Scope
One variable set, not a rebuild
Changes were grouped into a single coherent hypothesis so the result stayed interpretable. Testing twenty unrelated changes tells you something moved, but never why.
What the Test Delivered
A better page, and a repeatable method.
Efficiency
The same ad spend produced more registrations. No increase in budget, no new campaigns — just a page that did its job.
A template
The winning structure was rolled out across the remaining state campaigns, so one test improved more than one page.
Evidence
Decisions moved from opinion to data. The test gave a defensible answer and a baseline for the next round of optimization.
Takeaway

You can't optimize what you haven't tested. Opinions are cheap; split tests are proof.

The most valuable outcome wasn't the lift itself — it was having a documented, repeatable process for improving every paid page that came after it.
