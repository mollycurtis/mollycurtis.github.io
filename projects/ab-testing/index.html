Test it. Read it. Test it again.

Three rounds of A/B testing for an online course subscription business: state-specific paid search pages, the subscription checkout, and the homepage. Most changes won. The ones that didn't were just as useful.

01 · Paid Search

One template, tested in every state.

Every state runs its own paid campaign against a shared landing page. The redesigned template was tested across all of them. Most states improved. The few that dipped were tested in a month with no upcoming deadline, when intent runs lower, so timing explained the drop more than the design did.

The losses
West Virginia and Pennsylvania slipped, both tested in a month with no upcoming deadline. Their baselines were already high, so there was little room to gain, and lower intent that month did the rest.
The wins
Kentucky and New York started lowest and gained most. The template fixed pages that weren't working, not ones that already were.
What changed after
Winners shipped. The slips were re-tested in months with a deadline, rather than being written off from one slow window.

03 · Homepage

Top Categories, moved above the fold.

The category grid sat mid-page, below a product carousel, where few visitors reached it. Moving it directly under the hero put the most-used entry point where attention already was.

A · Quick links
Let people skip the hunt
Most visitors arrive knowing roughly what they need. Categories became the first thing after the hero, so they don't have to scroll to start.
B · Topic order
Order by demand, not catalog
The grid was reordered by what people actually browse, moving high-intent topics into the first row.
C · Hierarchy
Fewer blocks competing
Dropping the carousel that sat above it gave the categories a clear, uncontested slot.

The takeaway

Most pages improved. The exceptions were the point.

Across state landing pages, the checkout, and the homepage, every change was one hypothesis tested against real traffic. The pages that dipped weren't failures. They marked where a redesign wasn't the lever, or where timing mattered more than layout.
