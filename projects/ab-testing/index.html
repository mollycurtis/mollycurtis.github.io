01 — Paid Search

Nine states. One template. Nine different answers.

Each state ran its own paid campaign against a shared national page. I redesigned the template and tested it state by state — because assuming one market behaves like another is how you lose money at scale.

What the losses say
Illinois and West Virginia already converted well. The redesign replaced something those markets responded to — a reminder that a high baseline is a warning, not a target.
Where it won biggest
Kentucky and New York started lowest and gained most. The template fixed pages that weren't working; it didn't improve pages that already were.
What changed after
Winners rolled out. Losers reverted and got re-tested individually instead of forcing one national template onto every market.

Reading the result

New visitors drive the majority of the gain — expected, since navigation clarity matters most to people without a learned path through the site.

Returning-visitor samples are much smaller, so that segment's numbers carry less weight and shouldn't be read as a separate finding.

Gains held across desktop and mobile, and across both subscription and one-time purchasers.

The tradeoff
Subscription AOV fell about 13% — the cost of the discount, not a failure of the test. Revenue per visitor rose 33.2% and first-time subscribers rose 20.3%, which more than covers it. Blended AOV actually rose, though a handful of unusually large one-time purchases inflate that figure, so I don't lean on it. Not every metric has to go up for an experiment to be worth shipping.

A result you can't explain isn't a result.

01
One hypothesis
Changes tie to a single idea. Twenty unrelated edits tell you something moved, never why.
02
Segment before concluding
A flat overall number often hides a strong result in one segment and a weak one in another.
03
Read metrics together
CVR, RPV, ATC, and AOV as a set. A lift beside a drop is a tradeoff to weigh, not a win to announce.
04
Keep the losses
Three state redesigns lost. Those told me more about market differences than the six that won.

Optimization isn't a project. It's a habit.

The useful output here isn't one winning variant — it's a program where every result, good or bad, sets up the next question. The three states that lost changed how I roll anything out.
